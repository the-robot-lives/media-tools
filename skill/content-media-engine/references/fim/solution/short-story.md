# Short Story — a complete narrative arc in one sitting

A short story is a self-contained work of prose fiction that delivers a full
narrative arc — a character wants something, meets conflict, and is changed —
within a single sitting's read. Its craft is compression and unity: one central
conflict, a tight cast, a controlled point of view, and an economy that makes
every scene earn its place. Good short stories *show* through scene and detail and
land a resonant ending; bad ones summarize instead of dramatize, wander across too
many events, over-explain their own meaning, or stop without a turn. Readers
expect immersion, a consistent POV, dialogue that reveals character, and an ending
that recontextualizes what came before.

**Output form**: prose (markdown paragraphs; scene breaks allowed)  **Typical length**: 1,000–7,500 words
**Routed via**: `text_format: short-story` (chat-type generation)

## Genre Conventions & Structure

**The narrative arc (Freytag-derived):**

1. **Hook / opening** — establish POV character, voice, and situation fast; raise a
   question the reader wants answered.
2. **Inciting incident** — the event that disrupts the status quo and sets the
   central desire/conflict in motion (usually early — short fiction can't dawdle).
3. **Rising action** — escalating complications; each scene raises stakes or
   tightens the screw. Try/fail cycles.
4. **Climax** — the peak confrontation where the central conflict resolves one way
   or the other; the protagonist's decisive choice.
5. **Falling action / resolution (denouement)** — the immediate consequence and
   the new equilibrium; often very short in a short story.

Worked arc (a compact example the prep agent can pattern from):

```
Hook:      A retiree, Sam, waters his dead neighbor's garden every morning —
           we don't know why yet.
Incite:    The neighbor's estranged daughter arrives to sell the house.
Rising:    She wants him to stop; he keeps coming. Each visit reveals more —
           the garden was her mother's; Sam and the mother were close.
Climax:    She confronts him at the fence, demands the truth. He tells her.
Resolution:She waters the garden the next morning. Sam watches from his window.
```

One conflict, tiny cast, a turn (her understanding changes), a resonant final
image — no stated moral.

Short-story-specific conventions:

- **Single central conflict.** One dramatic question, not a saga. Subplots are
  minimal or absent.
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start as close to the inciting incident as possible;
  end as soon as the change has landed.
- **Small cast.** A protagonist, an antagonist/opposing force, maybe one or two
  others. Too many named characters fracture a short piece.
- **Scene vs. summary.** *Scene* = moment-by-moment, in real time, with sensory
  detail and dialogue (dramatize the important beats). *Summary* = compressed
  narration to bridge time (skip the boring parts). Alternate deliberately.
- **A turn / change.** The protagonist (or the reader's understanding) is different
  at the end. Something is at stake and something shifts.
- **Unity of effect (Poe).** Every element points toward one dominant impression.

**Point of view — choose one and hold it:**
- First person ("I") — intimate, voice-forward, limited to what the narrator knows.
- Third limited ("she/he," one head) — the modern default; close but flexible.
- Third omniscient — a knowing narrator moving between minds (harder to control in
  short form).
Do not head-hop (switch whose thoughts we're in) mid-scene without a break.

## Hard Constraints

1. **A complete arc** — inciting incident, rising action, a climax, and a
   resolution. Not a fragment or vignette-with-no-turn (unless the brief asks for a
   slice-of-life mood piece).
2. **One consistent POV and tense** throughout (first or third; past or present) —
   no unmarked head-hopping, no drifting between "I" and "she" for the same person.
3. **Length in the ~1,000–7,500-word band** (flash fiction < 1,000; anything past
   ~7,500 becomes a novelette). Respect any bound in the brief.
4. **A central conflict / dramatic question** established early and paid off.
5. **Scene-level dramatization** of the key beats (the climax must be a scene, not
   a summary).
6. **A resolution** — the story must *end*, not merely stop.

## How-To (worked recipes)

### How to open in the middle and hook fast
Start with the protagonist already in motion toward a want, or at the moment the
disruption hits — not with weather, backstory, or waking up. Bad: "Sarah was a
32-year-old accountant who lived alone and had always been shy." Good: "Sarah had
rehearsed the resignation speech for a week, and now, standing in Mr. Okafor's
doorway, she forgot the first line." We learn character *through* the charged
moment; backstory drips in later.

### How to show instead of tell
Convert states into evidence. Don't write "he was nervous"; write "he checked the
lock twice, then a third time, and still didn't believe it." Don't write "the room
was tense"; give the detail that makes it tense (the untouched coffee, the clock's
tick). Reserve direct telling for deliberate compression between scenes.

Before/after:

```
TELL:  Maria was devastated by the news and couldn't believe her father was gone.
SHOW:  Maria set the phone down on the counter, very carefully, as if it might
       break, and then stood there listening to the dial tone she had already
       ended. The kettle began to scream. She didn't move.
```

The reader *arrives* at "devastated" — you never say it.

### How to write dialogue that does work
Dialogue should reveal character, advance the conflict, or both — never just
transfer information. Give speakers distinct rhythms. Use subtext: people rarely
say what they mean ("Fine. It's fine." from someone who is not fine). Trim
greetings and small talk. Prefer plain "said" as the tag; let the *content* carry
the emotion, not adverb-laden tags ("she said angrily" → show the anger).

### How to control pacing with scene and summary
Dramatize the beats that matter (slow down: the confrontation, the decision) and
summarize the connective tissue (speed up: "The next three weeks passed in the
same grey routine"). A story that's all scene bloats; one that's all summary goes
cold. Alternate on purpose.

### How to land an ending
End on the *consequence of the change*, and stop soon after. Two strong short-story
endings: (1) the **resonant image** that crystallizes the theme; (2) the **turn**
that recontextualizes the opening. Avoid explaining the meaning ("And that's when
she realized life was about second chances"). Trust the story to have said it.

## Do's and Don'ts

### ✅ Do
- Establish POV, voice, and the dramatic question in the first paragraph or two.
- Keep one central conflict and a small cast.
- Dramatize key beats in scene; summarize the rest.
- Use concrete sensory detail and subtext-bearing dialogue.
- End on a turn or resonant image, then stop.

### ❌ Don't
- **Don't open with backstory, weather, or an alarm clock** — enter late.
- **Don't head-hop** or switch POV/tense without a marked break.
- **Don't summarize the climax** — the peak must be a lived scene.
- **Don't over-explain the theme** in a closing moral.
- **Don't overload names** — every extra named character taxes a short piece.
- **Don't resolve with coincidence** (deus ex machina) — the protagonist's choice
  should drive the climax.
- **Don't let adverbs and adjectives do the verbs' job** ("he ran quickly and
  angrily" → "he bolted").

## Tone, Voice & Register

Set by POV and character. Voice should be consistent and specific — a distinct
narrator or close-third filter colors word choice and observation. Prose register
ranges from spare (Carver, Hemingway) to lush (literary/lyrical) depending on the
brief; match it to the story's emotional key. Favor active voice and strong verbs.
Sentence rhythm is a tool — short sentences for tension, longer for reflection.

## Platform / Placement Constraints

Prose in paragraphs. Scene breaks marked with a blank line and a centered "* * *"
or "#" if multiple scenes. Dialogue on new paragraphs per speaker. Markdown is
fine for paragraphing; avoid headers *inside* a story. If serialized or excerpted,
respect the word bound and end on a coherent beat, not mid-scene.

## Common Pitfalls & Anti-patterns (incl. AI-tells)

- **Telling, not showing.** The dominant LLM failure: "She felt a deep sense of
  overwhelming sadness" instead of the image that conveys it. Convert emotion to
  evidence.
- **Summary masquerading as story** — the whole plot narrated from a distance with
  no lived scenes. Dramatize the key beats.
- **Head-hopping** — slipping into multiple characters' thoughts within one scene.
- **The tidy moral ending** — "And she learned that family was what mattered most."
  Cut it.
- **AI opening clichés**: "It was a cold, dark night," "Little did she know," waking
  from a dream, "In a world where..."
- **AI diction tells**: "delve," "a testament to," "navigate the complexities of,"
  "a tapestry of emotions," "in that moment, she realized," "a symphony of," "sent
  shivers down her spine," "the air was thick with tension." Ban these.
- **Em-dash and adverb overuse** — and adverb-stuffed dialogue tags ("she exclaimed
  breathlessly").
- **Too many characters / too many events** crammed into short form — the piece
  loses focus. One conflict.
- **No stakes** — nothing is risked, so nothing is felt.
- **Perfectly symmetrical, over-resolved endings** where every thread is bow-tied —
  short fiction often earns more from a single sharp turn than total closure.

## Prep-Agent Notes (media-tool specific)

From the raw creative brief:
1. **Fix the POV and tense** (default third-limited, past) and state them in the
   generation prompt — this prevents head-hopping.
2. **Extract the protagonist's want and the central conflict** → the dramatic
   question. Name the inciting incident and the climax beat in the prompt.
3. **State the target length** (word band) and the desired register/tone.
4. **Instruct "show, don't tell"** explicitly and list the AI-diction bans.
5. **Require a real climax scene and a turn/resonant-image ending** — forbid the
   explained moral.
6. **Keep the cast small** — tell the generator to limit named characters.
7. Fold `prompt.system` art-direction (mood, setting palette, theme) into voice,
   sensory detail, and the dominant impression — not into narrated commentary.
8. Output is prose (markdown paragraphs) via the chat provider path.
